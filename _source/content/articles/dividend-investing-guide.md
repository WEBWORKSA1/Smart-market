A dividend is a share of a company's profits paid out to shareholders, usually in cash and often every quarter. For many investors, a portfolio of dividend-paying stocks offers something appealing: a stream of income that does not require selling shares.

But dividend investing is not as simple as buying the highest yield you can find. A sustainable, growing dividend is usually worth more than a large one that gets cut. This guide covers the core metrics, walks through a 10-year example, and explains how to spot dividends that may not last.

## Key takeaways

- Dividend yield = annual dividend per share ÷ share price. It tells you current income, not total return.
- The payout ratio (dividends ÷ earnings) shows how much room a company has to keep paying and raising its dividend.
- Dividend growth compounds: a modest yield that grows steadily can eventually produce more income than a higher yield that stays flat.
- An unusually high yield is often a warning sign that the market expects a cut.
- Dividends are not guaranteed. Companies can reduce or suspend them at any time.

## How dividends work

When a company's board declares a dividend, it sets four dates:

- **Declaration date**: the dividend is announced.
- **Ex-dividend date**: you must own the stock before this date to receive the payment.
- **Record date**: the company checks its records to see who the shareholders are.
- **Payment date**: the cash arrives in your account.

On the ex-dividend date, a stock's price typically drops by roughly the dividend amount, all else equal, because new buyers will not receive that payment. That is why a dividend is not "free money": it is part of the company's value being handed to you in cash.

## The metrics that matter

### Dividend yield

**Dividend yield = Annual dividend per share ÷ Share price**

A $40 stock paying $1.60 a year yields 4%. Yield rises when the price falls, so a jump in yield can mean the stock dropped rather than the dividend grew.

### Payout ratio

**Payout ratio = Dividends per share ÷ Earnings per share**

A company earning $2.40 per share and paying $1.60 has a payout ratio of about 67%. Lower ratios leave a cushion if earnings fall. Very high ratios, especially above 100%, mean the company is paying out more than it earns, which is hard to sustain. Some businesses, such as real estate investment trusts (REITs), are structured to pay out most of their income, so compare within an industry. Many investors also check payout against free cash flow, since dividends are paid in cash.

### Dividend growth rate

This is how fast the dividend per share has risen, usually measured over five or ten years. A consistent record of increases through different economic conditions suggests management treats the dividend as a priority, though past increases do not ensure future ones.

### Yield on cost

**Yield on cost = Current annual dividend ÷ Your original purchase price**

It shows how your income has grown relative to what you paid. It is a useful motivational measure but not a valuation tool, because the market price, not your cost, determines what your money could earn elsewhere today.

## A 10-year worked example

Suppose you invest **$10,000** in a company trading at **$40** that pays a **$1.60** annual dividend and earns **$2.40** per share.

- Shares purchased: $10,000 ÷ $40 = **250**
- Starting yield: $1.60 ÷ $40 = **4%**
- First-year income: 250 × $1.60 = **$400**
- Payout ratio: $1.60 ÷ $2.40 = **66.7%**

Now assume the company raises its dividend **6% per year**, and you do not reinvest. Annual income would grow like this:

- Year 1: $400.00
- Year 3: $449.44
- Year 5: $504.99
- Year 8: $601.45
- Year 10: $675.79

By year 10 the dividend is about **$2.70** per share, so your yield on cost is roughly **6.8%**. Total dividends collected over the 10 years: about **$5,272**.

Compare that with a second stock yielding a flat **6%** with no growth. It pays $600 every year, or **$6,000** over 10 years. The growing dividend overtakes it in annual income in year 8, but the flat payer still collects more in total over the decade.

The lesson is not that one approach always wins. It is that a higher starting yield gives more income now, while growth gives more income later, and the trade-off depends on your time horizon. In practice, the bigger risk is usually that a very high yield gets cut, which would change the comparison entirely. At 6% annual growth, a dividend roughly doubles in about 12 years.

## Reinvesting dividends

Many brokers offer a dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP) that automatically uses each payment to buy more shares, often including fractional shares. Reinvesting adds a second layer of compounding: more shares produce more dividends, which buy more shares. It suits investors who do not need the income yet. Note that in a taxable account, reinvested dividends are generally still taxable in the year you receive them.

## Warning signs of an unsafe dividend

- **A yield far above peers.** The market may be pricing in a cut.
- **A payout ratio above 100%** of earnings or free cash flow for several periods.
- **Rising debt used to fund dividends** rather than operating cash.
- **Falling revenue or earnings** with no plan to reverse the trend.
- **Management language that softens commitments**, such as shifting from "growing the dividend" to "reviewing capital allocation."

## Building a diversified dividend portfolio

- **Spread across sectors.** Dividend payers cluster in utilities, consumer staples, financials and energy. Concentration can leave income vulnerable to one industry's downturn.
- **Mix yield and growth.** Combine some higher-yielding, slower-growing holdings with lower-yielding companies that raise payouts faster.
- **Consider funds.** Dividend-focused ETFs and mutual funds offer instant diversification. Compare expense ratios and how each fund selects holdings.
- **Mind taxes and account type.** Qualified and non-qualified dividends can be taxed differently. Holding income-heavy assets in tax-advantaged accounts may help, depending on your situation.
- **Focus on total return.** Dividends plus price change is what builds wealth. A stock that pays 5% but loses 10% a year is not a good income investment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are dividends guaranteed?

No. A board can raise, cut or suspend a dividend at any time. Even companies with long records of increases have reduced payouts during difficult periods.

### Is a higher dividend yield always better?

No. A very high yield often reflects a falling share price and market doubts about whether the dividend can be maintained. Sustainability matters more than size.

### What payout ratio is considered safe?

It varies by industry. For many ordinary companies, a ratio comfortably below 100% of earnings and free cash flow leaves room for error. REITs and some utilities routinely run higher ratios by design.

### Do I need to own the stock on the payment date?

No. You need to own it before the ex-dividend date. Selling after that date still entitles you to the upcoming payment.
