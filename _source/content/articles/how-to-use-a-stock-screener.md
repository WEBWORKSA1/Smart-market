There are thousands of publicly traded companies. Nobody can study them all. A stock screener solves that problem by letting you filter the market down to a short list of companies that meet criteria you choose, such as a reasonable valuation, a sturdy balance sheet or a steady dividend.

Used well, a screener is a time-saving research assistant. Used badly, it is a machine for finding cheap stocks that are cheap for good reasons. This guide shows you how to build sensible screens, read the results and turn a list of tickers into informed decisions.

## Key takeaways

- A screener narrows the universe; it does not tell you what to buy. Treat its output as a starting point for research.
- Combine at least three kinds of filters: valuation (what you pay), quality (how good the business is) and risk (how bumpy the ride may be).
- Avoid stacking too many filters. Overly tight screens return tiny lists that are often dominated by data quirks.
- Compare companies with peers in the same industry, since "normal" valuation ranges differ widely by sector.
- Always confirm screen data against the company's own filings before acting.

## What a stock screener does

A screener is a searchable table of companies with columns of data: price, market capitalization, P/E ratio, dividend yield, beta, recent price performance and more. You set minimum or maximum values for any column, and the screener hides everything that does not match.

## The filters that matter most

### Valuation: what you are paying

- **P/E ratio:** price divided by earnings per share. A ceiling such as "P/E below 25" weeds out the most expensive names, but compare within an industry.
- **Earnings yield:** earnings divided by price, the inverse of P/E. Easier to compare with bond yields.
- **Price-to-sales or price-to-free-cash-flow:** helpful when earnings are temporarily depressed or distorted.

### Size: how big the company is

- **Market capitalization:** share price times shares outstanding. Large companies tend to be more established and widely followed; smaller ones may grow faster but can be more volatile and less liquid.

### Stability and risk

- **Beta:** measures how much a stock has moved relative to the overall market in the past. A beta of 1.0 has moved roughly in line with the market; below 1.0 has been less volatile; above 1.0 more volatile. Beta is backward-looking and can change.
- **Debt levels:** debt-to-equity or similar ratios, where available, help avoid companies that could struggle if borrowing costs rise.

### Income

- **Dividend yield:** annual dividend divided by price. Set a sensible range rather than just a high minimum, because unusually high yields often signal that the market expects a cut.

### Momentum

- **Price performance:** returns over periods such as 3, 6 or 12 months. Momentum filters can help you avoid stocks in persistent decline, but past performance does not predict future results.

## Building a "quality at a fair price" screen

A balanced starting screen might look like this:

1. Market capitalization above a minimum you are comfortable with, to focus on established businesses.
2. P/E between roughly 5 and 25, excluding negative earnings and extreme outliers.
3. Beta below about 1.2, to limit the most volatile names.
4. Dividend yield between about 1% and 6%, if income matters to you.
5. Positive 12-month price performance, if you want to avoid falling knives.

These thresholds are illustrations, not rules. Adjust them to your goals and loosen any filter that wipes out an entire sector you want to consider.

## A worked example: checking one result

Suppose your screen returns a hypothetical company with these figures:

- Share price: $40
- P/E: 18
- Industry median P/E: 22
- Free cash flow per share: $2.00
- Beta: 0.9
- Dividend yield: 2.5%

A quick check:

- **Earnings yield** = 1 ÷ 18 = **5.6%** (rounded).
- **Relative valuation:** a P/E of 18 versus a peer median of 22 is about **18% below** the industry median (18 ÷ 22 − 1 = −18.2%).
- **Free cash flow yield** = $2.00 ÷ $40 = **5%**, equivalent to a price-to-free-cash-flow of **20**.
- **Beta** of 0.9 suggests the stock has historically moved a little less than the market.

So far, the company looks reasonably priced relative to its peers, with cash flow that supports its earnings. Now the real work begins: why is it cheaper than peers? Slower growth, a pending lawsuit, a weak segment or simply less investor attention could each explain the discount.

To see how a composite score works, imagine five factor scores of 70, 60, 80, 50 and 65. An equal-weighted average would be **65** out of 100. This is a simplified illustration of blending factors, not the exact Smart Score formula, which may weight and normalize factors differently.

## From shortlist to decision

Once you have 10 to 20 names, research each one:

1. **Read the latest annual report (10-K)** on SEC EDGAR, especially the business description and risk factors.
2. **Check recent earnings** for revenue growth, margins and cash flow trends.
3. **Understand the business model:** how the company makes money and what could disrupt it.
4. **Compare with two or three competitors** on the same metrics.
5. **Decide position size** in the context of your whole portfolio, not just this one idea.

## Common screening mistakes

- **Trusting stale or wrong data.** Screener data can lag or contain errors, especially after mergers, splits or restatements.
- **Value traps.** A low P/E can reflect falling future earnings.
- **Ignoring sector differences.** A single P/E ceiling across all industries will skew results toward banks and energy and away from software and healthcare.
- **Over-optimizing.** Tweaking filters until past winners appear builds a screen that fits history, not the future.
- **Screening once and forgetting.** Revisit your screens periodically; data and businesses change.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many filters should I use?

Three to six is a practical range for most beginners. Fewer filters keep your list broad enough to compare companies; more filters risk returning a handful of outliers.

### Is a high Smart Score a buy signal?

No. A composite score summarizes several metrics to help you prioritize research. It does not account for everything that matters, such as management quality, competitive position or upcoming events.

### Why do screen results change from day to day?

Prices move every trading day, and so do ratios built on price, such as P/E and dividend yield. Companies also report new earnings each quarter, which changes the underlying figures.
