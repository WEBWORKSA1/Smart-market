Hiring a financial advisor is a long-term relationship that can affect your savings for decades. Yet many people choose an advisor after a single friendly conversation, without asking how that person is paid or what they are legally required to do. A short, structured interview fixes that.

Below are 15 questions grouped into five themes. Bring them to every first meeting, ask for important answers in writing, and compare advisors side by side.

## Key takeaways

- Ask how the advisor is paid and request every fee in writing, expressed as an annual dollar amount.
- Confirm whether the advisor acts as a fiduciary for all of your accounts, all of the time.
- Verify registration and disciplinary history yourself using official databases rather than relying on the advisor's word.
- Clarify exactly what services you will get, how often you will meet, and who actually holds your money.
- A good advisor welcomes these questions; evasive or vague answers are a warning sign.

## Compensation and costs

### 1. How are you paid?

The answer should be specific: a percentage of assets, a flat fee, an hourly rate, commissions, or a mix. "The firm takes care of it" is not an answer. Understanding the model tells you where conflicts of interest may arise.

### 2. What will I pay in total each year, in dollars?

Ask the advisor to add up the advisory fee, the expense ratios of recommended funds, trading or platform fees, and any commissions. Converting percentages into dollars makes comparison much easier. For example, a 1% fee on a $250,000 portfolio is $2,500 a year before any fund costs.

### 3. Do you receive any payments from third parties?

Some advisors earn ongoing payments, referral fees or bonuses from fund companies or insurers. These are not necessarily improper, but you should know about them.

## Duties and conflicts of interest

### 4. Are you a fiduciary at all times, for all of my accounts?

Some professionals act as fiduciaries for advisory accounts but not when selling certain products. Ask for a yes or no, and ask them to confirm it in writing.

### 5. What conflicts of interest do you have, and how do you manage them?

Every compensation model has some conflict. An advisor paid on assets may discourage you from paying off debt; a commission advisor may favour products that pay more. Listen for a clear, honest answer.

### 6. Can I see your firm's disclosure documents?

In the US, ask for Form CRS (a short relationship summary) and, for investment advisers, Form ADV Part 2A (the firm brochure). In Canada, ask for the relationship disclosure information that explains fees, services and conflicts.

## Credentials and track record

### 7. What licenses and registrations do you hold?

Then check them yourself. In the US, search the SEC's [Investment Adviser Public Disclosure](https://adviserinfo.sec.gov/) site and FINRA's [BrokerCheck](https://brokercheck.finra.org/). In Canada, use the CSA's National Registration Search at [aretheyregistered.ca](https://www.aretheyregistered.ca/).

### 8. Have you ever been disciplined or had a customer complaint?

Ask directly, then compare the answer with what the databases show. A single old complaint may have a reasonable explanation; a pattern deserves serious caution.

### 9. What professional designations do you hold, and what did they require?

Designations vary widely in rigor. Some require exams, work experience and continuing education; others require little more than a short course. Ask what the letters mean and look up the issuing organization.

## Services and approach

### 10. What services are included, and what costs extra?

Is tax planning included? Estate planning coordination? Insurance reviews? Retirement income planning? Get a list so you know what you are paying for.

### 11. Who are your typical clients?

An advisor who mostly works with retirees may not be the best fit for a young family, and vice versa. Ask whether they have experience with situations like yours, such as self-employment income, stock compensation or cross-border issues.

### 12. How will you invest my money, and why?

Ask about their investment philosophy, how they decide on asset allocation, and how they handle market downturns. Be wary of anyone who promises to beat the market consistently or describes any investment as guaranteed to rise.

### 13. How often will we meet, and how quickly do you respond?

Agree on a review schedule, whether quarterly, twice a year or annually, and find out who you will deal with day to day: the advisor, an assistant, or a team.

## Custody and ending the relationship

### 14. Who holds my money?

In most arrangements, your assets are held by an independent custodian such as a brokerage firm, not by the advisor personally. You should receive statements directly from that custodian. This separation is an important safeguard.

### 15. What happens if I want to leave?

Ask about termination notice periods, account transfer fees, and any surrender charges on products such as annuities or certain insurance policies. Knowing your exit costs up front prevents surprises later.

## How to compare your answers

After meeting two or three advisors, put their answers in a simple table: total annual cost in dollars, fiduciary status, services included, meeting frequency and any red flags from the registration databases. The cheapest option is not automatically the best, but the cost should be clearly justified by the services you actually need.

Watch for these warning signs during any conversation:

- Pressure to decide or move money quickly.
- Reluctance to put fees or fiduciary status in writing.
- Promises of high returns with little or no risk.
- Requests to write cheques payable to the advisor personally.
- No record on the official registration databases.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it rude to ask an advisor how much they earn from me?

No. It is a standard, expected question. A professional advisor should be able to explain their compensation clearly and without hesitation.

### Should I bring documents to the first meeting?

A summary is usually enough for a first meeting: approximate account balances, debts, income and your main goals. Avoid sharing account passwords or full account numbers until you have verified the advisor and signed an agreement.

### How many advisors should I interview?

Speaking with at least two or three gives you a basis for comparison on cost, approach and personal fit.

### What if an advisor will not answer some of these questions?

Treat that as useful information. Transparency about fees, conflicts and history is a basic expectation, and an advisor who avoids these topics may not be the right choice.
