Inflation is the gradual rise in the general level of prices. When prices rise, each dollar buys a little less than it did before. A few percent a year rarely feels dramatic, but over a decade or two it can quietly shrink the real value of savings that sit still.

This guide explains how inflation works, shows a worked example of its long-term effect, and outlines practical ways to help your money keep pace.

## Key takeaways

- Inflation reduces purchasing power: at 3% a year, $1,000 today buys only about $553.68 worth of today's goods in 20 years.
- To keep the same purchasing power over that period, you would need about $1,806.11.
- The "real" return on your money is roughly your return minus inflation; a 2% savings rate with 3% inflation loses value in real terms.
- Holding cash for emergencies is sensible, but long-term savings usually need growth assets or inflation-linked investments to keep up.
- Your personal inflation rate can differ from the official figure depending on what you spend money on.

## What inflation is and how it is measured

Governments track inflation using a consumer price index (CPI), which measures the cost of a representative basket of goods and services such as food, housing, transportation and health care. In the US, the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes CPI data; in Canada, Statistics Canada does. Central banks, including the US Federal Reserve and the Bank of Canada, generally aim for inflation of around 2% over time.

Inflation is not the same every year. It can be low for long stretches and then rise sharply, as many households experienced in the early 2020s. That variability is one reason planning for it matters.

## Worked example: $1,000 over 20 years at 3% inflation

Assume prices rise by a steady 3% a year.

| Years from now | What $1,000 buys, in today's dollars | Dollars needed to match today's $1,000 |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | $1,000.00 | $1,000.00 |
| 10 | about $744.09 | about $1,343.92 |
| 20 | about $553.68 | about $1,806.11 |

The formulas are straightforward:

- **Future purchasing power** = amount ÷ (1 + inflation rate)^years. So $1,000 ÷ 1.03^20 ≈ $553.68.
- **Amount needed to keep pace** = amount × (1 + inflation rate)^years. So $1,000 × 1.03^20 ≈ $1,806.11.

In other words, money that does not grow loses about 45% of its purchasing power over 20 years at 3% inflation.

A quick mental shortcut is the **Rule of 72**: divide 72 by the inflation rate to estimate how many years it takes for prices to roughly double, or for cash to lose about half its value. At 3%, that is about 24 years.

## Nominal vs. real returns

Your **nominal** return is the percentage your money grows. Your **real** return is what is left after inflation. A simple approximation is:

> Real return ≈ nominal return − inflation rate

Suppose you keep $1,000 in a savings account paying 2% a year while inflation runs at 3%. After 20 years the account balance has grown, but its purchasing power in today's dollars is only about $822.73. You have more dollars, yet they buy less.

This is why the interest rate on savings should always be judged against inflation. Taxes on interest can widen the gap further, since tax is charged on the nominal gain.

## Ways to help protect purchasing power

There is no single investment that perfectly offsets inflation in every period, and every option carries trade-offs. These are common approaches people consider:

### Keep emergency cash, but not too much

An emergency fund belongs in safe, accessible accounts even though it may lose some value to inflation. The goal of that money is stability, not growth. Shop for competitive savings rates to narrow the gap, and avoid holding far more cash than you need for emergencies and near-term expenses.

### Diversified growth investments for long-term goals

Over long periods, diversified stock investments have historically grown faster than inflation, though they can fall sharply in the short term and past performance does not ensure future results. For goals many years away, a diversified mix of stocks and bonds suited to your timeline and risk tolerance is a common way to aim for positive real returns.

### Inflation-linked bonds

Some governments issue bonds whose principal or interest adjusts with inflation. In the US, examples include Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) and Series I savings bonds. These are designed to track official inflation measures, although their market prices can still fluctuate before maturity.

### Grow your income

Inflation affects what you earn as well as what you save. Negotiating raises, building skills, and reviewing pension or benefit indexing all help your income keep pace with rising costs.

### Review fixed costs and debt

Fixed-rate debt becomes easier to repay in real terms as prices rise, while variable-rate debt can become more expensive if interest rates go up in response to inflation. Knowing which kind you hold helps you plan.

## Your personal inflation rate

Official CPI reflects an average household. If you spend more on rent, groceries or health care than the typical basket, your costs may rise faster or slower than the headline number. Comparing your own spending year to year gives you a more accurate picture for budgeting and retirement planning.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is some inflation normal?

Yes. Many central banks target low, stable inflation of around 2% a year. Problems tend to arise when inflation is high or unpredictable.

### Should I move all my savings into investments to beat inflation?

Not necessarily. Money you may need soon, including your emergency fund, is usually better kept in stable accounts. Growth investments are generally more suitable for longer time horizons because of their short-term volatility.

### Does inflation affect retirement planning?

Significantly. Retirement can last decades, so planners usually assume rising costs over time. A budget that works today may need noticeably more dollars in 20 or 30 years.

### Can any investment remove inflation risk entirely?

No investment is guaranteed to beat inflation in every period. Be cautious of anyone who claims otherwise. Diversification and a plan matched to your timeline are the most practical tools.
