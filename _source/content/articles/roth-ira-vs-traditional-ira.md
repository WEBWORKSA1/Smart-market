An Individual Retirement Arrangement (IRA) is one of the most accessible retirement accounts in the United States. Anyone with taxable compensation can open one at a bank or brokerage, regardless of whether their employer offers a plan. The two main versions, Traditional and Roth, shelter investment growth from annual tax. What separates them is the timing of the tax bill: a Traditional IRA may give you a deduction now and taxes withdrawals later, while a Roth IRA takes after-tax money now and lets qualified withdrawals come out tax-free.

## Key takeaways

- Traditional IRA: contributions may be tax-deductible, and withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. Roth IRA: no deduction, but qualified withdrawals are tax-free.
- If your tax rate is the same when you contribute and when you withdraw, the two produce the same after-tax result.
- A Roth tends to win if you expect a higher tax rate later; a Traditional IRA tends to win if you expect a lower rate later.
- Roth IRAs have income limits for direct contributions and no required minimum distributions for the original owner. Traditional IRA deductibility can be limited if you have a workplace plan.
- Annual contribution limits, deduction phase-outs and Roth income limits change. Check the current figures on the IRS website before contributing.

## How each IRA works

### Traditional IRA

- **Contributions** may be fully, partly or not deductible. Deductibility depends on your income and on whether you or your spouse are covered by a workplace retirement plan.
- **Growth** is tax-deferred.
- **Withdrawals** are taxed as ordinary income. Withdrawals before age 59½ generally face an additional 10% tax on top of regular income tax, unless an exception applies.
- **Required minimum distributions (RMDs)** must begin once you reach the age set by current law. The IRS publishes the current starting age and calculation tables.

### Roth IRA

- **Contributions** are made with after-tax dollars and are not deductible.
- **Eligibility** to contribute directly phases out above certain income levels.
- **Qualified distributions** of both contributions and earnings are tax-free. Generally, that means you are at least 59½ and at least five tax years have passed since your first Roth IRA contribution.
- **Contributions (not earnings)** can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty, because that money was already taxed.
- **No RMDs** apply to the original owner during their lifetime.

### Rules both share

- You need taxable compensation (such as wages or self-employment income) to contribute. A spouse without earnings may be able to contribute based on the working spouse's income.
- There is one combined annual limit across all your Traditional and Roth IRAs, not a separate limit for each.
- Contributions for a tax year can generally be made up to that year's tax-filing deadline.

## The core math: tax rates decide

Compare the accounts by starting from the same amount of pre-tax earnings.

### Worked example

Assume $5,000 of pre-tax earnings, a 22% combined marginal tax rate today, and 7% annual growth for 30 years. For simplicity, assume the full Traditional contribution is deductible.

**Traditional IRA:** Contribute the full $5,000. After 30 years at 7%, it grows to about **$38,061**. Withdraw it at a 22% tax rate and you keep about **$29,688**.

**Roth IRA:** Pay 22% tax first, leaving $3,900 to contribute. After 30 years at 7%, it grows to about **$29,688**, all tax-free.

Identical. Now vary the tax rate in retirement:

| Tax rate at withdrawal | Traditional after tax | Roth after tax | Better choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12% | about $33,494 | about $29,688 | Traditional |
| 22% | about $29,688 | about $29,688 | Tie |
| 24% | about $28,927 | about $29,688 | Roth |

Two caveats. First, the Traditional result assumes you invest the tax saving from the deduction; if you spend it, the Roth looks better. Second, because the annual limit is the same dollar amount for both accounts, a dollar in a Roth is effectively "bigger": it represents more after-tax wealth than a dollar in a Traditional IRA. Savers who already contribute the maximum often find that this tilts the choice toward the Roth.

## The decision tree

Follow these steps in order.

1. **If your employer offers a 401(k) or similar match, then** contribute enough there to capture the full match before funding an IRA.
2. **If your income is above the Roth IRA limit, then** a direct Roth contribution is not available. Look into a nondeductible Traditional contribution, and read IRS guidance on Roth conversions, including the pro-rata rule that applies if you hold other pre-tax IRA money.
3. **If you are early in your career or in a low tax bracket, then** favour the Roth. You pay tax at today's low rate and lock in tax-free growth.
4. **If you are in your peak earning years and expect a noticeably lower income in retirement, then** favour the Traditional IRA, provided you can deduct the contribution.
5. **If you are covered by a workplace plan and your income makes the Traditional contribution nondeductible, then** the Roth is usually the stronger choice, because a nondeductible Traditional IRA gives you taxable earnings later without a deduction now.
6. **If you might need access to the money before retirement, then** the Roth offers more flexibility, since contributions can be withdrawn without tax or penalty.
7. **If you cannot predict your future tax rate, then** split your savings. Holding both pre-tax and Roth money lets you manage your taxable income each year in retirement.

## Factors people overlook

### Future tax law

Nobody knows what tax rates will be in 20 or 30 years. A Roth locks in today's rate; a Traditional IRA leaves you exposed to whatever rates apply later, for better or worse.

### Retirement income stacking

Traditional IRA withdrawals and RMDs add to your taxable income, which can increase the share of Social Security benefits subject to tax and can affect Medicare premium surcharges. Roth qualified distributions do not count toward those calculations.

### Heirs

Beneficiaries who inherit a Traditional IRA pay income tax on what they withdraw. Inherited Roth IRAs are generally tax-free to beneficiaries if the five-year rule is met, though most non-spouse beneficiaries must still empty the account within a set period.

### The Saver's Credit

Lower- and moderate-income savers may qualify for a federal tax credit for contributions to either type of IRA. Check the IRS for current eligibility.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I contribute to both a Roth and a Traditional IRA in the same year?

Yes, but your total contributions across both cannot exceed the single annual IRA limit.

### Can I have an IRA and a 401(k)?

Yes. Having a workplace plan does not stop you from contributing to an IRA, although it can limit whether a Traditional IRA contribution is deductible.

### What is a Roth conversion?

It is moving money from a pre-tax account, such as a Traditional IRA, into a Roth IRA. The converted amount is generally taxable in the year of conversion. Some people convert in low-income years to reduce future taxes.

### What if I contribute too much?

Excess contributions face a 6% excise tax for each year they remain in the account. Withdrawing the excess and any earnings on it before your tax-filing deadline generally avoids the tax.
