A 1% fee looks harmless on a statement. It is one cent on the dollar, and in a good year the market might return seven times that. But the fee is charged every year, on the entire balance, including all the growth you have already earned. Over decades, that quiet 1% can claim a quarter of your ending wealth. This guide shows the math in full, explains where fees hide, and sets out how to judge whether a fee is worth paying.

## Key takeaways

- On $100,000 growing at 7% before fees, a 1% annual fee leaves about $574,000 after 30 years, versus about $751,000 at 0.05%.
- That gap of roughly $176,000 is made up of fees actually charged plus the growth those fees would have earned.
- Compared with paying no fee at all, a 1% fee reduces the 30-year ending balance by about 24.5%; a 2% fee reduces it by about 43%.
- Fees appear in several places: fund expense ratios, advisory fees, trading costs, and sales loads.
- A higher fee can be worth paying for genuine advice, but you should know the dollar cost before you agree to it.

## The worked table

**Assumptions:** a one-time investment of $100,000, a 7% gross annual return, and the annual fee subtracted from that return each year (so a 1% fee means a 6% net return), compounded annually. No additional contributions, withdrawals or taxes. The 7% return is a planning assumption, not a forecast; real returns vary from year to year.

### Ending balance by annual fee

| Annual fee | Net return | After 10 years | After 20 years | After 30 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05% | 6.95% | $195,798 | $383,368 | $750,626 |
| 0.25% | 6.75% | $192,167 | $369,282 | $709,637 |
| 1.00% | 6.00% | $179,085 | $320,714 | $574,349 |
| 2.00% | 5.00% | $162,889 | $265,330 | $432,194 |

For reference, with no fee at all the same $100,000 would grow to $196,715, $386,968 and $761,226 over 10, 20 and 30 years.

### How much less you end up with than at 0.05%

| Annual fee | After 10 years | After 20 years | After 30 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25% | $3,631 | $14,086 | $40,989 |
| 1.00% | $16,713 | $62,654 | $176,277 |
| 2.00% | $32,908 | $118,038 | $318,432 |

### Share of the no-fee balance lost to fees

| Annual fee | After 10 years | After 20 years | After 30 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05% | 0.5% | 0.9% | 1.4% |
| 0.25% | 2.3% | 4.6% | 6.8% |
| 1.00% | 9.0% | 17.1% | 24.5% |
| 2.00% | 17.2% | 31.4% | 43.2% |

Notice how the share lost keeps rising with time. In the first decade, a 1% fee costs about 9% of the no-fee balance. By year 30, it costs about 24.5%. Time magnifies fees the same way it magnifies returns.

## Why the cost keeps growing

There are two parts to the cost of a fee.

**The fee itself.** In this model, the 1% fee adds up to roughly $79,000 actually charged over 30 years, because 1% is taken from a balance that keeps getting larger.

**The lost growth.** Every dollar taken in fees stops compounding. The money removed in year 5 would have grown for another 25 years. That missing growth accounts for the rest of the $186,876 shortfall compared with a no-fee account.

This is why comparing fees as percentages understates the difference. A fund charging 1.00% does not cost "about 1% more" than one charging 0.05%. Over 30 years in this example, it leaves you with about 23.5% less money.

## Where fees hide

Fees rarely appear as a single line. Check each of these:

- **Expense ratio.** The annual cost of running a mutual fund or ETF, deducted from the fund's assets. You will not see a bill; it reduces your return quietly. Broad index funds commonly charge well under 0.25%.
- **Advisory or wrap fee.** A percentage of assets charged by an advisor or managed account, often on top of the expense ratios of the funds held inside it.
- **Sales loads and commissions.** Front-end loads reduce the amount invested on day one; back-end loads or deferred sales charges apply when you sell.
- **Trading costs.** Bid-ask spreads and commissions, plus the internal trading costs of the fund itself, which are not included in the expense ratio.
- **Account and administration fees.** Annual account fees, transfer-out fees and inactivity fees.

In Canada, many mutual funds have historically carried higher management expense ratios (MERs) than comparable US funds, partly because some include trailing commissions paid to the selling dealer. Reading the Fund Facts document shows exactly what is included. In the US, a fund's prospectus fee table shows the same information.

## When a higher fee can be worth it

Paying for advice is not automatically a mistake. A good advisor may help with tax planning, retirement income strategy, insurance, estate planning, and, perhaps most valuable, keeping you from selling in a panic. Those services can be worth more than they cost.

The test is whether you know what you are paying and what you get in return. Questions worth asking:

1. What is my total annual cost, in dollars, including fund expenses and advisory fees?
2. Are you paid through commissions, a percentage of assets, a flat fee or an hourly rate?
3. Are you required to act in my best interest for all the advice you give?
4. What specific services are included beyond selecting investments?

If the answer to the first question is around 2% a year, the table above shows what that may mean over a working lifetime.

## Practical ways to cut fees

- Use low-cost index funds or ETFs for the core of your portfolio.
- Consider an all-in-one asset-allocation ETF or target-date fund instead of a managed account if you mainly need diversification and rebalancing.
- Look for flat-fee or hourly advice if you want planning help without an ongoing percentage of assets.
- In a US 401(k), check each fund's expense ratio; the plan's index options are often the cheapest.
- Hold investments in tax-advantaged accounts, such as an IRA or 401(k) in the US or a TFSA or RRSP in Canada, so taxes do not add another layer of drag.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a 1% advisory fee too high?

It depends on what is included. For investment selection alone, low-cost alternatives exist. For comprehensive planning, it may be reasonable, but compare the dollar cost with flat-fee options.

### Does a low fee mean a better fund?

Not on its own, but among funds tracking the same index, cost is one of the most reliable predictors of net returns. Lower cost leaves more of the market's return in your account.

### How do I find out what I am paying?

Look for the expense ratio or MER in the fund's prospectus or Fund Facts document, and ask your advisor or brokerage for a total annual cost in dollars.
