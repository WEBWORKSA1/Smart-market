401(k) Basics: How to Capture Every Dollar of Your Employer Match

Learn how a 401(k) works, how employer match formulas and vesting really work, and how to set your contribution so you never leave free match money behind.

By Smart.Market Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-11-086 min readHow we research

A 401(k) is a workplace retirement plan offered by many U.S. employers. You choose a percentage of each paycheck to contribute, the money is invested in funds you pick from the plan's menu, and it grows with tax advantages until retirement. Many employers also add money of their own through a "match." That match is part of your compensation, and missing it is one of the most common and avoidable mistakes in personal finance. This guide explains how the plan works and how to set your contribution so you collect every matching dollar on offer.

Key takeaways

  • A 401(k) lets you save for retirement directly from your paycheck, either pre-tax (Traditional) or after-tax (Roth, if your plan offers it).
  • An employer match is extra money your employer contributes when you contribute. Contributing less than the amount needed for the full match leaves pay on the table.
  • Read your plan's exact match formula, how often the match is calculated, and its vesting schedule.
  • Your own contributions are always yours. Employer contributions may vest over several years.
  • Annual contribution limits and catch-up limits are set by the IRS and change over time. Check the current figures on irs.gov.

How a 401(k) works

Contributions

You elect a percentage of pay, and your employer deducts it from each paycheck before you see it. Most plans offer one or both of these options:

  • Traditional (pre-tax) contributions reduce your taxable income today. Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.
  • Roth 401(k) contributions are made after tax. Qualified withdrawals in retirement, including the growth, are tax-free.

Investments

The plan offers a menu of funds, often including target-date funds that automatically shift from stocks toward bonds as your retirement year approaches. If you do not pick, your money typically goes into a default investment chosen by the plan.

Withdrawals

The account is designed for retirement. Withdrawals before age 59½ generally trigger regular income tax plus an additional 10% tax, unless an exception applies. Some plans allow loans or hardship withdrawals, but both reduce the money working for your future and can carry costs.

How the employer match works

A match is a formula. The two most common shapes look like this:

  • Partial match: "50% of your contributions, up to 6% of pay." You must contribute 6% to receive the full match, which equals 3% of your pay.
  • Dollar-for-dollar match: "100% of your contributions, up to 4% of pay." You must contribute 4% to receive the full match, which equals 4% of your pay.

Some employers use tiered formulas (for example, 100% on the first 3% and 50% on the next 2%) or make a fixed contribution regardless of what you put in. Your plan's Summary Plan Description spells out the exact rule.

Worked example

Assume a $60,000 salary and a match of 50% of contributions up to 6% of pay.

Your contribution You put in Employer adds Total per year
3% of pay $1,800 $900 $2,700
6% of pay $3,600 $1,800 $5,400

Contributing 3% instead of 6% leaves $900 a year of employer money uncollected. That sounds small, but if that $900 were invested each year at an assumed 7% annual return for 30 years, it would grow to about $85,000. Returns are never certain, so treat this as an illustration, not a forecast. The point is that the cost of skipping the match compounds.

Step-by-step: capture the full match

  1. Find your match formula. Look in your plan documents, benefits portal or ask HR. Note the percentage matched and the cap.
  2. Calculate the contribution needed. It is the cap in the formula. With "50% up to 6%," the number is 6%.
  3. If the plan matches per paycheck, then spread your contributions across the whole year. If you hit the annual IRS limit early (for example, by contributing a large percentage), you could miss matches on later paychecks unless your plan makes a year-end "true-up" contribution. Ask whether your plan does.
  4. If you were auto-enrolled, then check the default rate. Automatic enrollment often starts below the level needed for the full match.
  5. If cash is tight, then still try to reach the match before other investing goals. Few other options add money to your savings the moment you contribute.
  6. If you get a raise, then consider increasing your contribution percentage at the same time, so the higher rate never feels like a pay cut. Many plans offer automatic annual increases.

Vesting: when the match becomes yours

Your own contributions are always 100% vested. Employer contributions may follow a vesting schedule. Federal rules cap how long the schedule for matching contributions can be:

  • Cliff vesting: you own 0% until a set date, then 100%. For matching contributions, the cliff can be no longer than three years of service.
  • Graded vesting: ownership increases each year. For matching contributions, it must reach 100% by no later than six years of service.

Some employers vest immediately. If you are thinking of changing jobs, check how close you are to the next vesting milestone. Leaving a few weeks early can forfeit part of your match.

What to do after the match

Once you are collecting the full match, a common order of priorities is:

  1. Pay off high-interest debt such as credit cards.
  2. Build an emergency fund of several months of essential expenses.
  3. Consider an IRA or a Health Savings Account (HSA) if you are eligible, which may offer lower costs or wider investment choices than your plan.
  4. Raise your 401(k) contribution toward the annual limit as your budget allows.

Watch the fees

Every fund in your plan has an expense ratio, and some plans add administrative fees. Lower costs leave more of the return in your account. Your plan must provide a fee disclosure; compare funds before choosing.

Leaving your job

When you change employers, you can usually leave the money in the old plan, roll it into your new employer's plan, or roll it into an IRA. Cashing out triggers taxes and, if you are under 59½, likely the additional 10% tax. A direct rollover avoids mandatory withholding.

Frequently asked questions

Is the employer match taxed?

Employer matching contributions have traditionally gone into the pre-tax side of the plan and are taxed when you withdraw them. Some plans now allow employers to make matching contributions as Roth contributions; check your plan's options.

Should I choose Traditional or Roth 401(k) contributions?

Roth tends to make sense if you expect a higher tax rate in retirement; Traditional tends to make sense if you expect a lower one. Either way, the match works the same.

What if my employer does not offer a match?

A 401(k) can still be valuable for its tax advantages and automatic saving. Compare it with an IRA based on fees and investment options.

Can I contribute more after age 50?

Yes. The IRS allows additional catch-up contributions for workers age 50 and older. The amount is set each year, so check irs.gov for the current figure.
