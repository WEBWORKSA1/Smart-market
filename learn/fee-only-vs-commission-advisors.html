Fee-Only vs. Commission Financial Advisors: How to Choose

Learn how fee-only and commission financial advisors are paid, what fiduciary duty means, and how to check an advisor's record before you hire anyone.

By Smart.Market Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-11-087 min readHow we research

How a financial advisor gets paid shapes the advice you receive. Two advisors with the same credentials can steer you in very different directions if one earns a percentage of your assets and the other earns a commission on each product sold. Neither model is automatically good or bad, but you should know which one you are paying for, what it costs over time, and what obligations the advisor owes you.

This guide explains the main compensation models, what "fiduciary" means in plain terms, and how to verify an advisor in the United States and Canada before you sign anything.

Key takeaways

  • Fee-only advisors are paid directly by you; commission-based advisors are paid by the companies whose products they sell; fee-based advisors usually combine both.
  • A fiduciary duty generally requires an advisor to put your interests ahead of their own and to disclose conflicts of interest.
  • A 1% annual fee looks small but compounds: on $100,000 over 25 years it can cost roughly $90,000 in lost growth in a simple example.
  • Always look an advisor up on official registration databases before you share account details or sign an agreement.
  • Ask for every fee in writing, including fund expenses and any product commissions.

The main ways advisors are paid

Percentage of assets under management (AUM)

The advisor charges a yearly percentage of the money they manage, often deducted from your account quarterly. The appeal is alignment: if your account grows, the advisor earns more. The drawback is that the cost rises as your balance rises even if the work does not, and fees are charged in down years too.

Flat or retainer fees

You pay a fixed amount per year or per project, such as building a full financial plan. Costs are predictable and do not depend on your account size, which can be attractive for people with larger balances or those who want a plan rather than ongoing management.

Hourly fees

Some planners bill by the hour, much like an accountant or lawyer. This suits people who want a second opinion, a one-time review of their retirement plan, or help with a specific decision, and who are comfortable carrying out the recommendations themselves.

Commissions

The advisor is paid by a product provider when you buy something, such as an insurance policy, an annuity or certain mutual funds. You may not write a cheque, but the cost is built into the product. Commissions can make sense for a one-time purchase you would hold for many years, but they create an incentive to recommend products that pay more, or to recommend a product when none is needed.

Fee-based (hybrid)

"Fee-based" sounds similar to "fee-only" but is different. A fee-based advisor may charge you a planning or AUM fee and earn commissions on products. Always ask which accounts or products carry which kind of compensation.

What fiduciary duty means

In general terms, a fiduciary is required to act in your best interest, to avoid or clearly disclose conflicts of interest, and to provide advice that suits your situation. In the US, registered investment advisers owe a fiduciary duty to their clients. Broker-dealers recommending securities to retail customers must follow a "best interest" standard known as Regulation Best Interest, which is a different framework. In Canada, client-focused reforms require registered firms and individuals to address material conflicts in the client's best interest and to put the client's interest first when assessing suitability.

The practical lesson: titles like "financial advisor" or "wealth manager" are not reliable signals on their own. Ask the advisor directly, and in writing, whether they act as a fiduciary at all times and for all of your accounts.

A worked example: what a 1% fee costs

Suppose you invest $100,000 and it earns an assumed 6% per year before fees, with no further contributions, for 25 years.

Scenario Annual return after fees Value after 25 years
No advisory fee 6% about $429,187
1% annual AUM fee 5% about $338,635
Difference about $90,552

The fee does not just take 1% a year; it also removes the future growth that money would have earned. That does not mean a 1% fee is never worth paying. Good advice can help you avoid costly mistakes, manage taxes, and stay invested during downturns. It does mean you should know the number and decide whether the service justifies it. How to verify an advisor

Before you hire anyone, check their registration and history on official databases. It takes a few minutes and is free.

  • United States, investment advisers: Search the SEC's Investment Adviser Public Disclosure site at adviserinfo.sec.gov. You can read the firm's Form ADV, which describes fees, services, conflicts of interest and disciplinary history.
  • United States, brokers: Search FINRA BrokerCheck at brokercheck.finra.org for employment history, licenses and any customer complaints or regulatory actions.
  • Canada: Use the Canadian Securities Administrators' National Registration Search at aretheyregistered.ca to confirm the person and firm are registered and in which category.

Also ask for the firm's client relationship summary (in the US, Form CRS), which is a short plain-language document describing services, fees and conflicts. If an advisor is not registered, or is reluctant to share documents, treat that as a serious warning sign.

How to choose the right model for you

  • You want a plan, not ongoing management: Consider a flat-fee or hourly planner.
  • You want someone to manage investments over time: An AUM or retainer arrangement may fit; compare the total annual cost in dollars, not just the percentage.
  • You need a specific insurance product: A commission may be the normal way that product is sold, but get a second quote and ask how much the advisor earns.
  • You are unsure: Start with a single paid consultation and ask the questions in our companion guide before committing.

Frequently asked questions

Is a fee-only advisor always better than a commission advisor?

Not always. Fee-only arrangements reduce some conflicts of interest, but they still have costs and can carry their own incentives, such as encouraging you to keep more assets under management. What matters most is transparency, total cost and whether the advice fits your needs.

How do I find out exactly what I am paying?

Ask for a written breakdown that includes the advisory fee, the expense ratios of any funds, trading costs, and any commissions or ongoing payments the advisor receives. Then convert everything into an annual dollar figure.

Does a professional designation mean the advice will be good?

No. Designations such as CFP indicate training and an ethics commitment, but they do not replace checking registration, disciplinary history and the fee arrangement yourself.

Can I switch advisors if I am unhappy?

Yes. Accounts can generally be transferred to another firm. Ask about any account closing or transfer fees and any surrender charges on products before you move.
