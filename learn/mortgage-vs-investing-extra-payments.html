Pay Down the Mortgage or Invest? The Math on Extra Payments

Should extra cash go to your mortgage or into investments? See a worked $400,000 example, the break-even return, and the non-math factors that matter.

By Smart.Market Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-11-087 min readHow we research

If you have an extra few hundred dollars a month, you face a classic choice: send it to your mortgage or invest it. Paying down the mortgage gives you a certain return equal to your interest rate. Investing offers a potentially higher return, but with no promise attached. The right answer depends on your rate, your timeline, your taxes and how you feel about debt.

This guide walks through a worked example with real numbers, shows how to find the break-even return, and covers the factors a spreadsheet will not capture.

Key takeaways

  • Every extra dollar paid on a mortgage "earns" your mortgage interest rate, with certainty.
  • In our example, $300 a month extra on a $400,000, 5%, 25-year mortgage saves about $67,700 in interest and cuts about 4 years 11 months off the loan.
  • Investing the same $300 instead comes out ahead only if your after-tax investment return beats roughly the mortgage rate; in this example the break-even is about 4.9% a year.
  • Liquidity, emergency savings, higher-interest debt and employer retirement matches usually come before extra mortgage payments.
  • Many people split the difference, and that can be a reasonable choice.

The core idea: a certain return vs. an expected return

When you prepay principal, you avoid paying interest on that amount for the rest of the loan. That is economically similar to earning your mortgage rate on the money, and the saving does not depend on how markets perform. Investing in a diversified portfolio has historically offered higher long-run returns than many mortgage rates, but returns vary widely, can be negative for years at a time, and are never assured.

So the real comparison is: a known return equal to your mortgage rate versus an uncertain return that you hope will be higher.

Worked example: $400,000 at 5% over 25 years

Assumptions:

  • Mortgage: $400,000, 5% annual interest, 25-year amortization, monthly payments, interest calculated monthly.
  • Extra payment: $300 per month applied directly to principal from the first month.
  • Alternative: invest $300 per month at an assumed 6% annual return, compounded monthly, with no taxes or fees.

All figures below were calculated month by month.

Option A: Pay the extra $300 on the mortgage

Regular payments With $300 extra
Monthly payment $2,338.36 $2,638.36
Time to pay off 25 years (300 months) about 20 years 1 month (241 months)
Total interest about $301,508 about $233,810
Interest saved about $67,698

You are mortgage-free 59 months earlier. To make a fair comparison, assume that once the mortgage is gone you invest the full $2,638.36 you were paying, at the same assumed 6%, for the remaining 59 months. That builds an investment balance of roughly $180,537 by the end of year 25, with no mortgage.

Option B: Invest the $300 instead

Investing $300 a month for 300 months at an assumed 6% grows to roughly $207,898 by the end of year 25. Total contributions are $90,000. The mortgage is also paid off at year 25 on its regular schedule.

Comparing the two

At the end of year 25 Option A: prepay, then invest Option B: invest from the start
Mortgage balance $0 $0
Investment balance about $180,537 about $207,898

With a steady 6% return, investing comes out about $27,000 ahead. But change the assumption and the result flips. At a 4% return, Option A ends with about $171,710 and Option B with about $154,239, so prepaying wins.

The break-even return

Solving for the return at which both options finish equal gives roughly 4.9% a year in this example. That is close to the 5% mortgage rate, which makes sense: prepaying is essentially an investment that earns your mortgage rate. The small difference comes from the timing of cash flows in this set-up.

The key point is that the investing option needs to earn more than about your mortgage rate, after taxes and fees, to come out ahead. Use the mortgage calculator on this page to test your own balance, rate and extra payment.

Factors the math leaves out

Taxes

Investment returns may be taxed unless they are held in a tax-advantaged account. In the US, mortgage interest may be deductible if you itemize, which lowers your effective mortgage rate. In Canada, interest on a mortgage for your own home is generally not tax-deductible, which makes prepaying relatively more attractive compared with investing in a taxable account. Investing inside a TFSA, RRSP, IRA or 401(k) changes the comparison again.

Risk and sequence

A steady 6% each year is a simplification. Real portfolios rise and fall, and a downturn near the end of your timeline can leave you behind the prepayment option. Prepayment's saving does not depend on markets.

Liquidity

Money sent to your mortgage is hard to get back without refinancing or borrowing. Money in an investment account can be sold, although possibly at a loss. Build an emergency fund before making extra mortgage payments.

Prepayment rules

Many Canadian mortgages limit how much you can prepay each year without a penalty, and Canadian fixed-rate mortgages typically compound semi-annually rather than monthly, which changes the exact figures slightly. In the US, prepayment penalties are less common but do exist on some loans. Check your mortgage terms before paying extra.

Peace of mind

Some people value being debt-free for its own sake. That is a legitimate preference, and it can be worth a lower expected return.

A sensible order of priorities

Many people find this sequence helpful:

  1. Keep an emergency fund.
  2. Pay off high-interest debt such as credit cards.
  3. Capture any employer retirement match.
  4. Compare your mortgage rate with your realistic after-tax, after-fee expected return.
  5. If the gap is small or you value certainty, prepay or split extra money between both.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to make one lump-sum prepayment or monthly extra payments?

Both reduce interest. Earlier payments save more because they stop interest from accruing sooner. Check whether your lender has rules on prepayment amounts or timing.

Should I prepay if my mortgage rate is very low?

If your rate is well below what you can reasonably expect from investments after tax, investing may come out ahead on paper. You still accept market risk in exchange for that potential.

Does paying extra lower my monthly payment?

Usually not. Extra principal payments typically shorten the loan rather than reduce the required monthly payment, unless your lender recasts the loan.

What about splitting the money?

Splitting extra cash between the mortgage and investments is a practical way to balance certainty and growth potential, especially if you are unsure.
