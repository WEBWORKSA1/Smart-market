How to Read an Earnings Report in 10 Minutes

A practical 10-minute routine for reading a quarterly earnings report: revenue, margins, EPS, cash flow, guidance and the red flags most investors miss.

By Smart.Market Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-11-087 min readHow we research

Every quarter, public companies tell shareholders how the business performed. The headline number gets the attention, but the real story usually sits a few lines lower: in margins, cash flow and what management expects next. You do not need an accounting degree to read an earnings report well. You need a routine, a few ratios and the discipline to look past the press-release spin.

This guide gives you a repeatable 10-minute process you can apply to any company, plus a worked example so you can see each step with real numbers.

Key takeaways

  • Start with revenue growth and margins, not earnings per share (EPS). Sales and profitability trends are harder to dress up.
  • Compare results to the same quarter last year, not only to the prior quarter, to strip out seasonal effects.
  • Free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capital expenditures) tests whether reported profits are turning into actual cash.
  • Guidance and management commentary often move a stock more than the quarter itself.
  • Read the official filing on SEC EDGAR, not just the press release, when something looks unusual.

What is in an earnings report?

A US-listed company typically releases its quarterly results in a press release, often furnished to the SEC on Form 8-K, and then files a more detailed quarterly report on Form 10-Q (or an annual report on Form 10-K for the fourth quarter). Most companies also host a conference call where executives discuss results and answer analyst questions.

The three core financial statements are:

  • Income statement: revenue, costs and profit over the period.
  • Balance sheet: what the company owns (assets), owes (liabilities) and the shareholders' equity left over at a single point in time.
  • Cash flow statement: cash actually coming in and going out from operations, investing and financing.

The press release is written by the company and highlights what management wants you to notice. The 10-Q or 10-K contains the full statements, footnotes and risk disclosures.

The 10-minute routine

Minutes 1–2: Revenue and growth

Find total revenue (sometimes called net sales) and compare it with the same quarter a year earlier. Year-over-year comparison matters because many businesses are seasonal; a retailer's holiday quarter will almost always beat its spring quarter.

Ask: is growth accelerating, steady or slowing? If the company reports segments, check whether one division is carrying the others.

Minutes 3–4: Margins

Margins show how much of each sales dollar the company keeps.

  • Gross margin = gross profit ÷ revenue. Reflects pricing power and production costs.
  • Operating margin = operating income ÷ revenue. Adds in overhead such as salaries, marketing and research.
  • Net margin = net income ÷ revenue. Includes interest, taxes and one-time items.

Rising revenue with shrinking margins can mean the company is buying growth with discounts or rising costs. Flat revenue with expanding margins can signal efficiency gains, at least for a while.

Minutes 5–6: Earnings per share

EPS is net income divided by the number of shares outstanding. Use diluted EPS, which counts shares that could be created from stock options and convertible securities.

Many companies also report "adjusted" or non-GAAP EPS that excludes certain costs. Adjusted figures can be useful, but SEC rules require companies to reconcile them to the GAAP equivalent. Read that reconciliation. If the same "one-time" charges appear every quarter, they are not one-time.

Minutes 7–8: Cash flow

Open the cash flow statement and calculate free cash flow: cash from operations minus capital expenditures. Then compare it with net income. Over time, a healthy business generally converts a large share of its reported profit into free cash. Persistent gaps, where profits rise but cash does not, deserve a closer look at receivables, inventory and accounting policies.

Minutes 9–10: Guidance and the balance sheet

Look for forward guidance on revenue, margins or EPS for the next quarter or year. Note whether it was raised, maintained or cut. Then scan the balance sheet for changes in cash, total debt and inventory. A sharp jump in inventory relative to sales can be an early warning of slowing demand.

A worked example

Imagine a hypothetical company, Example Corp, reports the following for its latest quarter (figures in millions, except per-share data):

  • Revenue: $2,000, versus $1,800 in the same quarter last year
  • Gross profit: $900 (prior year: $774)
  • Operating income: $300 (prior year: $252)
  • Net income: $210
  • Diluted shares outstanding: 100
  • Operating cash flow: $280; capital expenditures: $80
  • Analysts' average EPS estimate going in: $2.00

Working through the routine:

  1. Revenue growth: ($2,000 ÷ $1,800) − 1 = 11.1% year over year.
  2. Gross margin: $900 ÷ $2,000 = 45%, up from $774 ÷ $1,800 = 43%.
  3. Operating margin: $300 ÷ $2,000 = 15%, up from $252 ÷ $1,800 = 14%.
  4. Net margin: $210 ÷ $2,000 = 10.5%.
  5. Diluted EPS: $210 ÷ 100 = $2.10, which is 5% above the $2.00 estimate.
  6. Free cash flow: $280 − $80 = $200, or about 95% of net income.

On these numbers, the quarter looks solid: double-digit growth, expanding margins and profits backed by cash. The final check is guidance. If management cut its outlook for next year despite this quarter, the stock could still fall, because prices reflect expectations about the future, not just the past.

Common red flags

  • Revenue growing much slower than receivables. Customers may be taking longer to pay, or sales may be pulled forward.
  • Recurring "non-recurring" charges. Restructuring costs every year shrink the credibility of adjusted EPS.
  • EPS growth driven mainly by buybacks. Fewer shares lift EPS even if net income is flat. Check whether total profit grew.
  • Tax-rate or one-off gains inflating net income. Compare operating income growth with net income growth.
  • Changes in accounting policies or auditor. These are disclosed in the filings and are worth reading carefully.

Frequently asked questions

Where can I find a company's official earnings filings?

US public companies file with the Securities and Exchange Commission. You can search any company's 8-K, 10-Q and 10-K filings for free on SEC EDGAR. Most companies also post results on their investor relations website.

Why did a stock fall after "beating" estimates?

Prices reflect expectations. A company can beat the average analyst estimate and still disappoint if guidance is weak, margins narrowed, or investors were expecting a bigger beat. The quarter is backward-looking; the share price is forward-looking.

Should I trust adjusted (non-GAAP) earnings?

Use them alongside GAAP figures, not instead of them. Read the reconciliation table and decide for yourself whether each excluded item is truly unusual.

How often should I review earnings reports for stocks I own?

Each quarter is a reasonable cadence for individual stocks. The goal is to confirm that the reasons you bought the company still hold, not to react to every short-term move.
