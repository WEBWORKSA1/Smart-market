How Big Should Your Emergency Fund Be?

Size your emergency fund in months of essential expenses, see a worked example, learn where to keep the cash and how to build it step by step without stress.

By Smart.Market Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-11-086 min readHow we research

An emergency fund is cash set aside for the expenses you cannot plan for: a job loss, a car repair, a medical bill, an urgent trip home. It is the foundation that keeps a surprise from turning into credit card debt or forcing you to sell investments at a bad time. The usual guideline is three to six months of expenses, but the right size for you depends on how stable your income is and how many people rely on it. This guide shows how to calculate your number, where to keep it, and how to build it.

Key takeaways

  • Measure your emergency fund in months of essential expenses, not total spending or income.
  • Three months is a common floor for stable, dual-income households; six months or more suits single earners, the self-employed and people in volatile industries.
  • Keep the money safe and accessible, such as in a high-interest savings account at an insured institution, not in stocks.
  • Build it in stages: a small starter buffer first, then the full target.
  • Review the target once a year, because inflation and life changes raise the cost of your essentials.

Step 1: Add up your essential monthly expenses

An emergency fund only needs to cover the bills you must keep paying during a crisis. Leave out discretionary spending such as dining out, subscriptions and vacations, which you would cut in a real emergency.

Include:

  • Rent or mortgage payment
  • Groceries
  • Utilities, phone and internet
  • Transportation (car payment, fuel, transit)
  • Insurance premiums
  • Minimum payments on debts
  • Childcare and essential medical costs

Step 2: Choose your number of months

Use this decision tree to pick a target.

  1. If you have a stable job, a second household income and few dependants, then aim for about 3 months of essentials.
  2. If you are the only earner in your household, then aim for about 6 months.
  3. If you are self-employed, work on commission, freelance or have seasonal income, then aim for 6 to 12 months, because income gaps can be longer and less predictable.
  4. If you work in an industry prone to layoffs or would need a long time to find similar work, then lean toward the higher end of your range.
  5. If you own a home or an older car, then add a buffer for repairs, or keep a separate sinking fund for predictable maintenance.
  6. If you have health conditions or dependants with special needs, then consider a larger fund to cover out-of-pocket costs.

Worked example

Suppose your essential monthly expenses look like this:

Expense Monthly cost
Rent $1,500
Groceries $500
Utilities and phone $200
Transportation $300
Insurance $150
Minimum debt payments $250
Total essentials $2,900
  • 3-month fund: $2,900 × 3 = $8,700
  • 6-month fund: $2,900 × 6 = $17,400

How long will it take to build? Saving $400 a month, you would reach the 3-month target in about 22 months (21.75, rounded up). Saving $600 a month, you would reach the 6-month target in 29 months.

Why the target creeps up: If prices for your essentials rise 3% a year, the same 6-month cushion would cost about $19,013 after three years. That is why the target should be recalculated annually, not set once and forgotten. Use the inflation calculator on this page to see how your own target changes over time.

Step 3: Decide where to keep it

The emergency fund has two jobs: be there when you need it, and be worth what you expect. Growth comes third.

Good options:

  • High-interest savings accounts at banks or credit unions covered by deposit insurance (CDIC or a provincial insurer in Canada; FDIC or NCUA in the United States). Confirm that your institution and account type are covered.
  • Money market accounts offered by insured banks.
  • Short-term deposits such as cashable GICs or no-penalty CDs, for a portion you are unlikely to need immediately.

Poor options for emergency money:

  • Stocks or stock funds. Markets can fall sharply at the same time as layoffs rise, which is exactly when you might need the cash.
  • Locked-in deposits with heavy early-withdrawal penalties.
  • Your chequing account. It is too easy to spend, and it usually earns little or nothing.
  • Anything promoted as "guaranteed" high returns. High promised returns with no risk are a classic warning sign of fraud.

Canadians often hold an emergency fund in a TFSA savings account, because interest earned is tax-free and withdrawals restore contribution room the following year. In the United States, interest in a regular savings account is generally taxable.

Step 4: Build it without stalling

  1. Start with a starter buffer. Even $500 to $1,000 covers many small emergencies and breaks the cycle of borrowing for them.
  2. Automate it. Schedule a transfer to savings on payday, so saving happens before spending.
  3. Use irregular money. Tax refunds, bonuses and gifts can make large jumps toward your goal.
  4. Balance it with debt payoff. If you carry high-interest debt, many people split extra cash between the starter fund and debt, then finish the full fund after the debt is gone.
  5. Keep collecting any employer retirement match. A workplace match is usually worth continuing while you build savings.

Using and rebuilding the fund

Before you dip in, ask three questions: Is it unexpected? Is it necessary? Is it urgent? A planned vacation or a new phone usually fails that test; a broken furnace in winter passes it.

After you use the fund, pause other extra savings goals and rebuild it first. Then set a recurring reminder to review your target every year or after any major life change, such as a move, a new child or a change in jobs.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to pay off debt or build an emergency fund?

Most people do both in stages: a small starter fund first so a surprise does not create new debt, then focus on high-interest debt, then finish the full emergency fund.

Can a credit card or line of credit be my emergency fund?

Available credit can be a backup, but it is not a substitute. Lenders can reduce limits, and borrowing during a crisis adds interest costs when your income may be lowest.

Should my emergency fund be invested to beat inflation?

Not in stocks. The goal is stability and access. A high-interest savings account can offset part of inflation while keeping the money safe and available.

Is six months always enough?

No. Six months is a guideline. Self-employed people and households with one income in a volatile field may want more.
