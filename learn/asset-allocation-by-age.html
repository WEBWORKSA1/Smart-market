Asset Allocation by Age: Building a Portfolio That Fits Your Life

How to split your portfolio between stocks, bonds and cash at each stage of life, with age-based rules of thumb, a worked rebalancing example, and their limits.

By Smart.Market Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-11-086 min readHow we research

Asset allocation is the split of your portfolio between broad types of investments, mainly stocks, bonds and cash. It drives most of the ups and downs you will experience as an investor, far more than which individual fund you pick. Age is a useful starting point for choosing that split, because it shapes how long your money has to recover from a downturn. But it is only a starting point. This guide covers the common rules of thumb, shows what different mixes do in a decline, and explains how to keep your allocation on track.

Key takeaways

  • Stocks offer higher expected long-run growth with larger swings; bonds and cash offer lower expected returns with more stability.
  • Rules like "110 minus your age in stocks" give a reasonable first draft, not a final answer.
  • In a hypothetical 30% stock decline with bonds flat, a $100,000 portfolio that is 90% stocks falls to $73,000; one that is 40% stocks falls to $88,000.
  • Your time horizon, income stability and tolerance for losses matter as much as your birth year.
  • Rebalancing once a year, or when your mix drifts by about five percentage points, keeps risk where you intended it.

The three building blocks

Stocks (equities) represent ownership in companies. Over long periods they have delivered the highest returns of the major asset classes, but they can lose a large share of their value in a bad year and take years to recover.

Bonds (fixed income) are loans to governments or companies that pay interest. High-quality bonds have usually been less volatile than stocks and have often, though not always, held up better during stock market declines. Both fell together in some years, such as 2022, when interest rates rose sharply.

Cash and cash equivalents include savings accounts, money market funds and short-term government bills. They are stable in nominal terms but may not keep pace with inflation over long periods.

Why age matters

A 25-year-old saving for retirement may not touch the money for 40 years. That leaves time to ride out several bear markets and keep buying at lower prices along the way. A 63-year-old who plans to start withdrawals in two years has far less room. A large loss just before or early in retirement, when withdrawals begin, can permanently reduce how long a portfolio lasts. This is often called sequence-of-returns risk.

So the usual pattern is to hold more stocks when young and shift gradually toward bonds and cash as the date you need the money approaches.

Age-based rules of thumb

The best-known shortcuts subtract your age from a fixed number to get your stock percentage. The rest goes to bonds and cash.

Age 100 minus age 110 minus age 120 minus age
25 75% stocks 85% stocks 95% stocks
40 60% stocks 70% stocks 80% stocks
55 45% stocks 55% stocks 65% stocks
65 35% stocks 45% stocks 55% stocks

The original "100 minus age" rule dates from a time of shorter life expectancies and higher bond yields. Many planners now consider it too conservative for people who may spend 25 to 30 years in retirement, which is why the 110 and 120 versions are common. Target-date funds follow a similar idea through a pre-set "glide path" that reduces stocks over time.

Where the rules fall short

A rule based on age alone ignores things that matter just as much:

  • Time horizon for each goal. Money for a home purchase in three years should be invested conservatively, whatever your age.
  • Income stability. A tenured employee or someone with a defined-benefit pension can often take more stock market risk than a self-employed person with variable income. A pension or government benefits, such as Social Security in the US or CPP and OAS in Canada, act somewhat like a bond holding.
  • Loss tolerance. If a 30% drop would lead you to sell, a more conservative allocation you can hold is better than an aggressive one you abandon.
  • Portfolio size relative to needs. Someone whose savings far exceed their spending needs may choose either more or less risk, depending on goals.

What different mixes do in a downturn

Consider $100,000 split between stocks and bonds. Suppose stocks fall 30% and bonds hold their value. This is a simplified scenario for illustration.

Allocation (stocks/bonds) Value after decline Loss Gain needed to recover
90 / 10 $73,000 27% 37.0%
60 / 40 $82,000 18% 22.0%
40 / 60 $88,000 12% 13.6%

Note the last column. Losses and recoveries are not symmetrical: a 27% loss needs a 37% gain to get back to even. The more you have lost, the harder the climb back.

A more aggressive mix has historically produced higher long-run growth, which is its reward for these bigger drawdowns. The right choice is the one where you can tolerate the bad years and still benefit from the good ones.

Rebalancing: keeping your mix on target

Markets move your allocation without asking. Rebalancing brings it back.

Worked example. You hold $100,000 in a 60/40 mix: $60,000 in stocks and $40,000 in bonds. Over a year, stocks gain 20% and bonds are flat.

  • Stocks are now worth $72,000; bonds remain $40,000; total $112,000.
  • Your stock share has drifted to about 64.3%.
  • To restore 60/40, the targets are $67,200 in stocks and $44,800 in bonds.
  • You would sell $4,800 of stocks and buy $4,800 of bonds, or simply direct new contributions to bonds until the mix is back on target.

Rebalancing makes you trim what has risen and add to what has lagged. Common approaches are to rebalance once a year or whenever an asset class drifts more than about five percentage points from its target. Rebalancing inside tax-advantaged accounts, such as an IRA or 401(k) in the US or a TFSA or RRSP in Canada, avoids triggering capital gains tax on the trades.

Putting it into practice

  1. Write down each goal and when you will need the money.
  2. Use an age rule to set a first-draft stock percentage for long-term goals.
  3. Adjust it for your income stability, pensions and how you reacted to past market drops.
  4. Implement it with low-cost, diversified index funds, or a single asset-allocation ETF or target-date fund.
  5. Rebalance on a schedule and review the allocation every few years or after major life changes.

Frequently asked questions

Should I move entirely to bonds at retirement?

Usually not. Retirement can last decades, and an all-bond portfolio risks losing ground to inflation. Many retirees keep a meaningful share in stocks.

Are target-date funds a good way to handle allocation?

They can be. They set an age-appropriate mix and rebalance automatically. Check the fees and whether the glide path matches your risk tolerance.

How often should I change my allocation?

Gradual shifts every few years, or after major life events such as a new job, marriage or approaching retirement, are typical. Changing it in reaction to market news tends to hurt results.
