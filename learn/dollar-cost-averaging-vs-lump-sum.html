Dollar-Cost Averaging vs. Lump Sum: What the Math Says

Should you invest a windfall all at once or spread it out? Worked examples show when a lump sum wins, when dollar-cost averaging helps, and how to decide.

By Smart.Market Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-11-086 min readHow we research

You have a lump of cash to invest: an inheritance, a bonus, the proceeds of a home sale. Do you put it all in today, or feed it into the market in equal pieces over the next year? The first approach is lump-sum investing. The second is dollar-cost averaging (DCA). The math and the psychology point in different directions, and understanding both is the key to choosing well.

Key takeaways

  • Because markets have tended to rise over time, investing a lump sum immediately has historically beaten spreading it out in roughly two-thirds of periods studied.
  • Dollar-cost averaging wins when prices fall during the investing period, and reduces the regret of investing just before a drop.
  • Assuming 7% expected stock returns and 3% on cash, spreading $12,000 over 12 months gives up about $220 in expected value versus investing at once.
  • Investing a fixed amount from every paycheque is a form of dollar-cost averaging that most people do by default, and it is a sound habit.
  • The best plan is one you will actually follow, written down before you start.

The two approaches defined

Lump sum: invest the whole amount as soon as it is available.

Dollar-cost averaging: split the amount into equal parts and invest them at regular intervals, for example $1,000 a month for 12 months, holding the rest in cash until it is invested.

DCA has one mechanical feature people like: because each purchase is a fixed dollar amount, you buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when they are high. Your average cost per share ends up slightly below the average of the prices you paid.

Worked example: three markets, two strategies

Suppose you have $12,000 to invest in a fund priced at $100 on day one. Either you buy 120 shares immediately, or you invest $1,000 at the start of each month for 12 months. These price paths are hypothetical, chosen to show how each strategy responds.

Market path Price at month 12 Lump-sum shares Lump-sum value DCA shares DCA value Better result
Steady rise $115 120.00 $13,800 112.20 $12,903 Lump sum by $897
Steady fall, partial recovery $90 120.00 $10,800 137.39 $12,366 DCA by $1,566
Sharp dip, then rally $112 120.00 $13,440 123.23 $13,802 DCA by $362

In the rising market, waiting cost money: every month spent in cash meant buying at a higher price. In the falling market, DCA bought most of its shares cheaply and came out well ahead. In the volatile market, DCA bought heavily during the dip, giving it an average cost of about $97.38 per share against an average price of $98.33.

The example shows that neither strategy wins every time. The question is which is more likely to come out ahead.

What the long-run math says

The expected-value argument

Over long periods, stocks have tended to return more than cash. If that holds, every dollar waiting on the sidelines is expected to earn less than a dollar already invested.

Here is the calculation with explicit assumptions: stocks expected to return 7% a year, cash earning 3% a year, both compounded monthly, over 12 months.

  • Lump sum: $12,000 fully invested grows to an expected $12,840.
  • DCA: $1,000 moved from cash into stocks each month grows to an expected $12,618.
  • Expected cost of DCA: about $222, or roughly 1.8% of the amount invested.

The gap widens if cash earns less, if expected stock returns are higher, or if you spread purchases over a longer period.

What historical studies have found

Several studies of historical data from the US and other developed markets have found that investing a lump sum immediately beat a 12-month DCA schedule in roughly two-thirds of rolling periods, for both all-stock and balanced portfolios. That matches the expected-value argument: when markets rise more often than they fall, getting invested sooner usually helps.

Roughly a third of the time, though, DCA came out ahead. Nobody knows in advance which kind of year is coming.

Why dollar-cost averaging still makes sense for some people

The math compares expected outcomes. People live through actual outcomes, and the experience of watching a large sum fall 20% in the first month can push investors to sell at the worst time.

DCA can be the better choice when:

  • Regret would derail you. If a drop right after investing would make you abandon the plan, a slower entry that keeps you invested may produce a better real-world result than a mathematically superior plan you quit.
  • The amount is large relative to your existing portfolio. A windfall that triples your invested assets is a bigger emotional event than one that adds 5%.
  • You are moving into a riskier allocation for the first time. Easing in gives you time to learn how you react to volatility.

A middle path many people use is to invest half immediately and spread the rest over three to six months. It captures much of the expected benefit of investing early while limiting the worst-case regret.

The DCA you are probably already doing

If you contribute part of every paycheque to a 401(k), IRA, TFSA or RRSP, you are dollar-cost averaging. That is not a strategic choice between two options; it is simply investing money as you earn it. It is one of the most reliable habits in personal finance, and nothing in the lump-sum research argues against it.

The lump-sum versus DCA question only applies when you already hold the cash.

How to decide

  1. Confirm the money is long-term. Cash needed within a few years should not go into stocks by either method.
  2. Choose your target allocation first. The mix of stocks and bonds matters more than the entry schedule.
  3. Pick a method and write it down, including the exact dates and amounts if you choose DCA.
  4. Automate the schedule so you are not deciding each month whether now feels like a good time.
  5. Do not pause because of headlines. Stopping DCA after a drop removes its main benefit.

Frequently asked questions

Is dollar-cost averaging a bad strategy?

No. It has a lower expected return than investing a lump sum immediately when markets rise over time, but it reduces short-term regret and can help nervous investors stay the course.

How long should a DCA schedule last?

Many people use 3 to 12 months. Longer schedules leave more money in cash for longer, which increases the expected cost.

Should I wait for a market drop before investing a lump sum?

Waiting for a drop is market timing. Prices may rise further before any decline, and the drop you wait for may never take prices below today's level.
