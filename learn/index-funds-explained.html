Index Funds Explained: Why Most Pros Can't Beat Them

What index funds are, how they work, why most actively managed funds trail them over time, and how to choose a low-cost index fund or ETF for your portfolio.

By Smart.Market Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-11-086 min readHow we research

An index fund does something that sounds unambitious: instead of trying to find the winning stocks, it buys all the stocks in a market index and holds them. Yet over long periods, this simple approach has outperformed most professional fund managers. This guide explains how index funds work, why the math favours them, and what to look for when choosing one.

Key takeaways

  • An index fund holds the securities in a market index, such as the S&P 500 or a total-market index, in roughly the same proportions.
  • Before costs, the average actively managed dollar earns the market return; after costs, it must earn less. Index funds keep costs close to zero.
  • S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA scorecards have repeatedly found that most actively managed funds trail their benchmarks over 10- and 15-year periods.
  • On $10,000 over 25 years at a 7% gross return, a 0.75% fee costs about $8,100 compared with a 0.05% fee.
  • Index funds do not protect against market declines. They deliver the market's return, including its losses.

What an index fund actually is

A market index is a list of securities chosen by a published rule. The S&P 500 tracks 500 large US companies, weighted by market value. The S&P/TSX Composite does something similar for Canada. Total-market and international indexes cover thousands of companies.

An index fund, available as a mutual fund or an exchange-traded fund (ETF), buys the securities in its index so that its performance mirrors the index. When the index changes, the fund changes. There is no team trying to predict which companies will do best.

Mutual fund or ETF?

Both can track the same index. ETFs trade on an exchange throughout the day like a stock, and often have very low expense ratios. Index mutual funds are bought and sold at the end-of-day price and sometimes allow automatic investments in exact dollar amounts. For a long-term investor, the cost and the index matter far more than the wrapper.

Why most professionals fall behind

The arithmetic of active management

In 1991, economist William F. Sharpe set out a simple argument. Everyone who owns stocks, taken together, owns the whole market. So before costs, the average dollar invested by active managers must earn the same return as the market, because active and passive investors together are the market.

Costs then decide the outcome. Active funds pay for research, analysts and frequent trading, and they charge investors higher fees to cover it. Index funds keep those costs minimal. So after costs, the average actively managed dollar must trail the average index dollar. This is not a forecast; it follows from the arithmetic.

A worked version: suppose the market returns 7% in a year. The average active dollar also earns 7% before costs. If active funds charge 1% in fees and trading costs, the average active investor keeps about 6%. An index fund charging 0.05% keeps about 6.95%.

What the scorecards show

Some active managers do beat the market in a given year. The difficulty is that few keep doing it. S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes SPIVA (S&P Indices Versus Active) scorecards for the US, Canada and other regions. Year after year, they have found that a large majority of actively managed funds trail their benchmark index over long periods, and that past outperformance is a weak predictor of future outperformance.

That second point matters. Even if a skilled manager exists, picking them in advance is hard, and the funds that topped the charts over one five-year period often fall back in the next.

The cost difference in dollars

Fees are charged every year, on your whole balance, whether the fund gains or loses. Over decades, the gap compounds.

Take $10,000 invested for 25 years at a 7% gross annual return:

Annual fee Net return Value after 25 years
0.05% 6.95% $53,644
0.75% 6.25% $45,522
Difference $8,122

The higher-fee fund would need to beat the index by 0.70 percentage points every single year, after its own trading costs, just to break even. The SPIVA data suggests most funds do not manage it over long periods.

What index funds do not do

Index funds are not a shortcut to safe returns. They own the market, so when the market falls, they fall with it. A US large-company index fund can lose a third or more of its value in a bad year. Index investing works over long horizons partly because it keeps investors fully exposed to those swings.

They also carry some specific trade-offs:

  • Concentration. Market-value-weighted indexes put more money in the largest companies, so a handful of firms can make up a large share of the fund.
  • No downside management. A manager will not move to cash ahead of a downturn. (Evidence that active managers can do this reliably is weak.)
  • Home bias. A single-country index leaves out most of the world's companies. Many investors pair domestic and international index funds.

How to choose an index fund

  1. Pick the market you want to own. A total US or total world fund is broad; an S&P 500 or S&P/TSX 60 fund is narrower. Most portfolios combine domestic and international exposure.
  2. Compare expense ratios. Among funds tracking similar indexes, lower is usually better. Broad index funds commonly charge well under 0.25% a year.
  3. Check tracking difference. A good fund's return should sit very close to its index's return, minus the fee.
  4. Confirm there are no sales loads or account fees that undo a low expense ratio.
  5. Hold it in the right account. Use an IRA or 401(k) in the US, or a TFSA or RRSP in Canada, where possible to reduce taxes on growth.

Frequently asked questions

Are index funds safe?

They are diversified, which reduces the risk of any single company failing. They are not protected from market declines, and their value can fall sharply in the short term.

Is an S&P 500 fund diversified enough?

It covers 500 large US companies across many industries, but no small companies, no bonds and no international stocks. Many investors add international and bond funds for broader diversification.

Do index funds pay dividends?

Yes. Most pass through the dividends paid by the companies they hold. You can usually choose to receive them as cash or reinvest them automatically.

Can an index fund go to zero?

For a broad market index to go to zero, essentially every company in it would have to become worthless at the same time. That is a very different risk from owning a single stock.
