TFSA vs. RRSP: The Decision Tree for Canadians

TFSA or RRSP? Use this step-by-step decision tree, a worked tax example and plain-English rules to choose the right account for your next Canadian dollar.

By Smart.Market Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-11-087 min readHow we research

The Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) and the Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP) both let investments grow without annual tax on interest, dividends or capital gains. The difference is when the tax is paid: an RRSP gives a deduction now and taxes withdrawals later, while a TFSA takes already-taxed money and pays nothing on the way out. Which one wins depends mostly on your tax rate today versus your tax rate when you withdraw.

Key takeaways

  • An RRSP gives you a tax deduction today; withdrawals are taxed as income later. A TFSA gives no deduction, but qualifying withdrawals are tax-free.
  • If your marginal tax rate is the same now and at withdrawal, the two accounts produce the same after-tax result.
  • RRSPs tend to win when your income is high now and expected to be lower later; TFSAs tend to win when your income is modest now or you need flexibility.
  • TFSA withdrawals do not count as income, so they do not reduce income-tested benefits such as the Guaranteed Income Supplement or trigger the Old Age Security recovery tax.
  • Contribution room for both accounts changes over time. Check your personal limits in your CRA My Account or on your Notice of Assessment before contributing.

How each account works

The RRSP in brief

  • Contributions are deductible from your taxable income, which can produce a refund.
  • Room is based on a percentage of your previous year's earned income, up to an annual maximum, minus any pension adjustment from a workplace pension. Unused room carries forward.
  • Withdrawals are added to your income and taxed at your marginal rate. Tax is withheld at source when you withdraw, and the room you used is generally gone for good.
  • Deadline: you must convert your RRSP to a Registered Retirement Income Fund (RRIF) or an annuity by the end of the year you turn 71.
  • Special programs: the Home Buyers' Plan and Lifelong Learning Plan let you borrow from your RRSP for specific purposes and repay over time.

The TFSA in brief

  • Contributions are not deductible.
  • Room accumulates each year from the year you turn 18 (if you are a resident with a valid SIN), and unused room carries forward indefinitely.
  • Withdrawals are tax-free, and the amount you withdraw is added back to your room on January 1 of the following year.
  • No age limit on contributing or holding the account, and no forced withdrawals.

Both accounts charge a penalty tax on over-contributions, generally 1% per month on the excess. Track your room carefully, especially if you have accounts at more than one institution.

The core math: why tax rates decide the winner

The cleanest way to compare the accounts is to start from the same amount of pre-tax income and follow it all the way through.

Worked example

Assume you have $10,000 of pre-tax earnings, a 30% marginal tax rate today, and investments that grow 6% a year for 25 years.

RRSP route: You contribute the full $10,000 because the deduction offsets the tax. After 25 years at 6%, it grows to about $42,919. When you withdraw it at a 30% tax rate, you keep about $30,043.

TFSA route: You pay 30% tax first, leaving $7,000 to contribute. After 25 years at 6%, it grows to about $30,043. Withdrawals are tax-free, so you keep all of it.

Same answer. When the tax rate is identical at both ends, the accounts are mathematically equivalent. Now change one variable:

Tax rate at withdrawal RRSP after tax TFSA after tax Better choice
20% about $34,335 about $30,043 RRSP
30% about $30,043 about $30,043 Tie
40% about $25,751 about $30,043 TFSA

Note one assumption hiding in the RRSP result: it only works if you actually invest the tax refund (or contribute the "grossed-up" amount). If the refund is spent, the RRSP's advantage shrinks or disappears.

The decision tree

Work through these steps in order. Stop at the first one that clearly applies.

  1. If you have high-interest debt (credit cards, payday loans), then pay that down first. Eliminating interest of 20% or more is a certain return that few investments can match.
  2. If your employer offers a group RRSP or pension match, then contribute at least enough to collect the full match. Matching money is an immediate return on your contribution.
  3. If you do not yet have an emergency fund, then build one, and consider holding it in a TFSA. You can withdraw without tax and get the room back next year.
  4. If you are buying your first home within a few years, then look at the First Home Savings Account (FHSA), which combines a deduction on the way in with tax-free qualifying withdrawals, and the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan. Read the CRA rules for both before deciding.
  5. If your marginal tax rate is low today (for example, early in your career, a student, or on parental leave), then favour the TFSA. A deduction is worth less at a low rate, and you can save RRSP room for higher-income years.
  6. If your marginal tax rate is high today and you expect lower income in retirement, then favour the RRSP and invest the refund.
  7. If you expect to rely on the Guaranteed Income Supplement or to have retirement income near the OAS recovery threshold, then lean toward the TFSA, because RRSP/RRIF withdrawals count as income for those tests and TFSA withdrawals do not.
  8. If you are unsure about future tax rates, then split contributions between both. Tax diversification gives you choices about which account to draw from later.

Situations that change the answer

Workplace pensions

A workplace pension reduces your RRSP room through the pension adjustment and adds taxable income in retirement. Members of generous pensions may face similar tax rates in retirement, which tilts the math toward the TFSA.

Spousal RRSPs

If one partner earns much more, a spousal RRSP lets the higher earner take the deduction while the funds are eventually taxed in the lower earner's hands, subject to attribution rules on withdrawals made within a few years of a contribution.

Estate planning

On death, an RRSP or RRIF is generally taxed as income in the final return unless it rolls over to a spouse or another qualifying beneficiary. A TFSA can pass to a successor holder or beneficiary without the balance being taxed as income, although growth after death can be taxable.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Treating the RRSP refund as a bonus. Spending it erases much of the RRSP's edge.
  • Re-contributing a TFSA withdrawal in the same year without enough unused room, which triggers the over-contribution penalty.
  • Withdrawing from an RRSP in a high-income year. The withdrawal is taxed at your highest marginal rate.

Frequently asked questions

Can I have both a TFSA and an RRSP?

Yes. Many people use the RRSP for retirement and the TFSA for flexible goals and retirement top-ups.

What happens if I contribute too much?

Both accounts charge a penalty tax on excess contributions, generally 1% per month for as long as the excess remains. Withdraw the excess promptly and check the CRA guidance on how to report it.

Where do I find my contribution room?

Your RRSP deduction limit appears on your latest Notice of Assessment. Both RRSP and TFSA room are shown in CRA My Account, but the TFSA figure may not include transactions from the current year.

Is the TFSA only for short-term savings?

No. A TFSA can hold stocks, bonds, ETFs and GICs, and it can be a strong retirement account.
