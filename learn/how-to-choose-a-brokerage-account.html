How to Choose a Brokerage Account: A 7-Point Checklist

Compare brokerage accounts with a 7-point checklist covering SIPC and CIPF protection, fees, account types, fractional shares, research tools and support.

By Smart.Market Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-11-087 min readHow we research

A brokerage account is where you buy and hold investments such as stocks, exchange-traded funds (ETFs), mutual funds and bonds. Opening one is usually quick, but the account you pick will hold your savings for years, so it is worth comparing a few options carefully.

This checklist covers the seven points that matter most for most beginners. It does not recommend any specific firm; instead, it shows you what to look for so you can judge any brokerage yourself.

Key takeaways

  • Confirm the firm is a member of the relevant investor protection fund: SIPC in the US or CIPF in Canada.
  • Protection funds cover missing assets if a member firm fails, not investment losses from falling markets.
  • Small fees add up; check account, trading, fund, currency conversion and transfer-out costs.
  • Make sure the brokerage offers the account types you need, such as retirement or tax-advantaged accounts.
  • Fractional shares, useful research tools and reachable customer support can matter as much as price.

1. Regulation and investor protection

Before anything else, confirm the brokerage is properly registered and a member of the investor protection fund in your country.

United States: SIPC. The Securities Investor Protection Corporation protects customers of member broker-dealers if the firm fails financially and customer assets are missing. Coverage is up to $500,000 per customer per capacity, which includes up to $250,000 for cash. SIPC does not protect against losses because an investment's value fell, and it does not cover every product. You can check a firm's membership on sipc.org and look up the firm on FINRA BrokerCheck.

Canada: CIPF. The Canadian Investor Protection Fund protects eligible client property held by member firms if a member becomes insolvent. Coverage limits apply per account category; for example, general accounts combined are covered up to $1 million, with separate limits for categories such as registered retirement accounts. The current categories and limits are listed on cipf.ca. You can also confirm a firm's registration through the CSA's National Registration Search.

Like SIPC, CIPF does not cover market losses, unsuitable investments or poor advice. It is a safety net for firm failure, not a promise that your investments will hold their value.

2. Fees and costs

Many brokerages now advertise low or zero commissions on stock and ETF trades, but trading commissions are only one cost. Check for:

  • Account maintenance or inactivity fees, sometimes waived above a minimum balance.
  • Fund expense ratios on ETFs and mutual funds, which are charged by the fund, not the broker, but still reduce your return.
  • Currency conversion fees, especially relevant for Canadians buying US-listed securities.
  • Transfer-out fees if you later move your account elsewhere.
  • Margin interest rates if you ever borrow against your account.
  • Fees for paper statements, wire transfers or phone-assisted trades.

A simple test: estimate what you would pay in a typical year based on how often you plan to invest and what you plan to buy.

3. Account types

Make sure the brokerage offers the accounts that fit your goals. Common examples include:

  • United States: individual and joint taxable accounts, traditional and Roth IRAs, and custodial accounts for minors. Some firms also offer accounts for self-employed retirement plans.
  • Canada: non-registered accounts, Tax-Free Savings Accounts (TFSAs), Registered Retirement Savings Plans (RRSPs), First Home Savings Accounts (FHSAs) and Registered Education Savings Plans (RESPs), depending on the firm.

If you think you may need a particular account type later, it is easier to choose a brokerage that already offers it than to transfer later.

4. Investment choices and fractional shares

Check whether the brokerage offers what you actually plan to buy: individual stocks, ETFs, index mutual funds, bonds, or GICs and certificates of deposit. Some platforms focus on a narrow set of products.

Fractional shares let you buy part of a share with a small dollar amount. This makes it easier to invest a fixed amount each month and stay fully invested even when a single share is expensive. Ask whether fractional investing is available for both stocks and ETFs, and whether automatic recurring purchases are supported.

5. Research and educational tools

Good tools help you make informed decisions without encouraging excessive trading. Look for:

  • Clear fund information, including expense ratios, holdings and historical performance.
  • Portfolio views that show your overall asset allocation.
  • Educational articles or courses written in plain language.
  • Tax documents and cost-basis reporting that are easy to download.

Be cautious of platforms that emphasize game-like features or constant notifications. These can nudge you toward frequent trading, which often increases costs and mistakes.

6. Ease of use and security

You should be able to deposit money, place an order and find your statements without confusion. Test the website and mobile app if possible. On the security side, look for two-factor authentication, login alerts, and clear policies on how the firm handles unauthorized access.

7. Customer support

When something goes wrong, such as a transfer stuck in progress or a tax form error, you will want a real person to help. Check:

  • Support hours and channels: phone, chat, secure messaging.
  • Whether support is available in your language.
  • How the firm handles account transfers and complaints.

Putting the checklist to work: an example

Suppose you plan to invest $200 a month into a broad-market ETF in a tax-advantaged account. Your priorities would likely be: investor protection membership, availability of that account type, no commission on ETF purchases, automatic recurring purchases with fractional shares, and no inactivity fee. Over a year you would make 12 purchases totalling $2,400. Even a $5 commission per trade would cost $60 a year, which is 2.5% of what you contributed, so in this case low or zero trading costs matter a great deal. For a larger, infrequent investor, other features may matter more.

Frequently asked questions

Is my money safe if my brokerage goes out of business?

If the firm is a SIPC member (US) or CIPF member (Canada), eligible assets are protected up to the published limits if they are missing when the firm fails. In many cases, customer accounts are transferred to another firm. This protection does not cover declines in market value.

Are zero-commission brokerages really free?

Not entirely. Firms earn money in other ways, such as interest on uninvested cash, margin lending, currency conversion and other fees. Read the fee schedule to understand your actual costs.

Can I have more than one brokerage account?

Yes. Some people use different firms for different account types or goals. Keep track of contribution limits for tax-advantaged accounts across all firms.

How long does it take to open an account?

Online applications often take minutes, though identity verification and funding may take a few business days.
