How Much Do You Really Need to Retire? A Number-First Guide

Estimate your retirement number with the 4% rule: how it works, where it came from, its limits, and a worked example adjusted for pensions, CPP and Social Security.

By Smart.Market Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-11-087 min readHow we research

"How much do I need to retire?" has no universal answer, but it does have a method. Start with what you expect to spend each year, subtract the income you will receive from pensions and government benefits, and then work out how large a portfolio is needed to cover the gap for the rest of your life. The most widely used shortcut for that last step is the 4% rule. This guide explains the rule, where it came from, why it is only a starting point, and how to turn it into a personal target.

Key takeaways

  • The 4% rule suggests you can withdraw about 4% of your portfolio in the first year of retirement, then adjust that dollar amount for inflation each year, with a high historical likelihood of the money lasting about 30 years.
  • Flipped around, it means your target portfolio is roughly 25 times the annual spending your savings must cover.
  • Subtract reliable income such as a workplace pension, CPP and OAS, or Social Security before multiplying, which can shrink the target dramatically.
  • The rule is based on historical U.S. market returns and a 30-year horizon. Longer retirements, lower future returns, high fees or rigid spending call for a more conservative rate.
  • Your number is an estimate, not a promise. Revisit it every few years as your plans and markets change.

Step 1: Estimate your retirement spending

Begin with spending, not income. Retirement budgets often differ from working budgets: commuting costs and retirement contributions disappear, while travel, hobbies and health care costs may rise. Two practical approaches:

  • Bottom-up: list expected annual costs for housing, food, transportation, insurance, health care, travel, gifts and taxes.
  • Replacement rate: use a percentage of your pre-retirement income as a rough first guess, then refine it with a bottom-up budget as retirement gets closer.

Remember taxes. If you will withdraw from pre-tax accounts such as an RRSP/RRIF, Traditional IRA or 401(k), your spending figure should include the income tax on those withdrawals.

Step 2: Understand the 4% rule

Where it came from

The 4% rule traces back to research published in the 1990s, most notably by financial planner William Bengen. He tested how much a retiree could withdraw from a mix of U.S. stocks and bonds, increasing withdrawals each year with inflation, across every historical 30-year period in his data. A first-year withdrawal of about 4% survived even the worst starting years in that record, including retirements that began just before severe market declines or periods of high inflation. Later academic studies, often referred to as the Trinity study, examined similar questions and reached broadly similar conclusions for 30-year periods.

How it works in practice

  1. In year one, withdraw 4% of your starting portfolio.
  2. Each following year, withdraw the same dollar amount adjusted for the prior year's inflation, regardless of how the portfolio performed.

For example, on a $1,500,000 portfolio, you would withdraw $60,000 in year one. If inflation runs at 3%, you would withdraw $61,800 in year two and $63,654 in year three.

The multiply-by-25 shortcut

Because 4% is one twenty-fifth, you can estimate a target by multiplying annual spending from savings by 25. A more cautious 3.5% withdrawal rate corresponds to multiplying by about 28.6; a 3% rate corresponds to multiplying by about 33.3.

Step 3: Subtract reliable lifetime income

Most retirees do not fund everything from a portfolio. Income sources that continue for life, especially if they rise with inflation, reduce how much you must save:

  • Canada: Canada Pension Plan (CPP) or Quebec Pension Plan (QPP) and Old Age Security (OAS), plus any workplace pension.
  • United States: Social Security, plus any workplace pension.
  • Other income: annuities, rental income or part-time work, if reliable.

Get personal estimates rather than guessing. Canadians can view CPP contribution history and estimates through My Service Canada Account; Americans can view Social Security estimates through a my Social Security account. Benefit amounts depend on your earnings history and the age you start.

Worked example

Assume you expect to spend $60,000 a year in retirement, in today's dollars, including taxes.

Without any other income:

  • Target = $60,000 × 25 = $1,500,000.

With government benefits and pensions:

Suppose your combined CPP and OAS, or your Social Security, is estimated at $24,000 a year.

  • Gap to cover from savings = $60,000 − $24,000 = $36,000 a year.
  • Target at 4% = $36,000 × 25 = $900,000.
  • Target at a more cautious 3.5% = $36,000 ÷ 0.035 = about $1,028,571.

Accounting for $24,000 of benefits cut the target by $600,000. That is why getting a real benefit estimate is one of the most valuable steps in retirement planning.

How much to save each month: Reaching $900,000 in 25 years at an assumed 6% annual return, compounded monthly, requires saving about $1,299 a month. Reaching $1,500,000 under the same assumptions requires about $2,165 a month. Returns vary, so treat these as planning estimates.

Limitations of the 4% rule

The rule is a useful anchor, but it rests on assumptions that may not match your situation.

  • Historical U.S. data. Past returns do not guarantee future ones, and other countries' markets have had weaker histories.
  • A 30-year horizon. Someone retiring at 50 may need their money to last 40 years or more, which argues for a lower withdrawal rate.
  • Fees and taxes. The original research did not deduct investment fees. A 1% annual fee meaningfully reduces a sustainable withdrawal rate.
  • Rigid spending. Real retirees often cut back after a bad market year. Flexible spending rules, such as skipping inflation increases after losses, can support a higher starting rate; rigid spending needs a lower one.
  • Asset mix. The research assumed a substantial allocation to stocks. A portfolio held mostly in cash or bonds may not support 4%.
  • Sequence of returns. Large losses in the first few years of retirement do more damage than the same losses later, because withdrawals lock them in.
  • Uneven spending. Many people spend more early in retirement and face higher health care costs late in life.

A decision tree for choosing your withdrawal rate

  1. If you plan to retire at a traditional age with a roughly 30-year horizon and a balanced portfolio, then 4% is a reasonable starting assumption.
  2. If you plan to retire early or have a family history of longevity, then consider 3% to 3.5%.
  3. If your portfolio carries high fees, then lower your rate or reduce the fees.
  4. If most of your essential spending is already covered by pensions and government benefits, then you may tolerate a somewhat higher rate on the remaining discretionary spending.
  5. If you are willing to trim spending in down years, then a flexible strategy can let you start a little higher.

Frequently asked questions

Is the 4% rule still valid?

It remains a common planning benchmark, but many planners now use a range rather than one number, depending on expected returns, retirement length and spending flexibility.

Should I use today's dollars or future dollars?

Using today's dollars for spending and an inflation-adjusted (real) return for growth keeps the math consistent and easier to understand.

Does the 4% rule include taxes?

No. It describes gross withdrawals. Build expected income taxes into your spending figure.

What if I fall short of my number?

Common levers include working a little longer, delaying government benefits, saving more, reducing planned spending or continuing part-time work. Each can shrink the gap significantly.
